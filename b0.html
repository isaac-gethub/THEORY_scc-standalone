Primary Goals of SAP Controls & Compliance

Before starting B1–B10, get grounded in why SAP controls exist at all. Every module in this programme ties back to one or more of the seven goals below.

1. Regulatory Compliance

  • SOX (Sarbanes-Oxley) — ICFR (Internal Control over Financial Reporting), ensuring financial statements are free of material misstatement
  • Industry-specific mandates — GDPR (data privacy), HIPAA (healthcare), Basel III (banking), local statutory requirements
  • External audit readiness — providing auditors with evidence controls operate effectively, avoiding audit findings/qualified opinions

2. Fraud Prevention & Detection

  • Segregation of Duties (SoD) — no single user can execute conflicting steps in a process end-to-end (e.g., create vendor + post payment)
  • Sensitive access controls — restricting powerful transactions (mass changes, direct table updates, debug/change access) to authorized, monitored users
  • Detective controls — anomaly monitoring, exception reports, continuous controls monitoring (CCM) to catch fraud that preventive controls miss

3. Financial Statement Integrity

  • Accuracy, completeness, and validity of transactions posted to the ledger
  • Proper authorization hierarchies (approval limits, workflow release strategies)
  • Reliable cutoff, valuation, and reconciliation processes

4. Operational Risk Management

  • Protecting against unauthorized or erroneous transactions that disrupt business operations
  • Business continuity — ensuring critical processes (O2C, P2P, R2R) aren't compromised by access or config errors
  • Data integrity across master data (vendor, customer, material, GL accounts)

5. IT General Controls (ITGCs) — the technical backbone

  • Change management — controlled transport path, no direct production changes
  • Access management — provisioning/deprovisioning aligned to role, periodic access recertification
  • Computer operations — job scheduling, batch monitoring, backup/recovery

6. Accountability & Traceability

  • Audit trails (change documents, table logging) that answer "who did what, when, and was it authorized"
  • Clear ownership of controls (control owners, risk owners) so failures have accountability

7. Stakeholder Confidence

  • Investor and board assurance that governance is sound
  • Reduced insurance/regulatory risk exposure
  • Reputational protection — control failures often become public failures
